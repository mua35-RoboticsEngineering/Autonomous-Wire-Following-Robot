# Autonomous Wire-Following Robot

An autonomous wire-following mouse robot developed during a University of Bath engineering group project week.

The objective of the project was to design, build, test, and optimise a small autonomous robot capable of following an energised track wire and completing a race course as quickly and reliably as possible.

The system combined inductive sensing, analogue signal conditioning, embedded control, PWM motor control, power electronics, mechanical integration, simulation, laboratory testing, and iterative engineering development.

---

## Project Overview

The Mouse Race project required our team to develop an autonomous robot capable of detecting and following a wire carrying an approximately 20 kHz electrical signal.

Rather than using optical line-following sensors, the robot detected the magnetic field generated by the current flowing through the track wire.

A pick-up coil generated a small AC voltage in response to this magnetic field. The signal was then filtered, amplified, rectified, and converted into a voltage that could be interpreted by the microcontroller.

An Arduino Nano ESP32 processed the sensor information and controlled two DC motors through MOSFET-based motor drive circuits. Motor speeds were adjusted using pulse width modulation (PWM), allowing the robot to steer by varying the relative speed of the left and right wheels.

The final robot used a two-wheel drive configuration with a low-friction rear skid.

The main engineering objectives were to:

## System Operation

The autonomous mouse operates through several connected subsystems.

### 1. Track Wire Detection

Current flowing through the race track wire generates an alternating magnetic field.

An inductive pick-up coil mounted underneath the robot detects this field and produces a small AC voltage. The strength of this signal varies depending on the robot's position relative to the wire.

### 2. Signal Conditioning

Because the voltage generated by the pick-up coil is very small, the signal cannot be used directly by the microcontroller.

The signal-conditioning system uses:

- A passive band-pass filtering stage.
- Operational amplifier circuitry.
- Precision rectification.
- Peak detection.
- Smoothing.

The band-pass filter is designed around the approximately 20 kHz track frequency so that the desired signal can be isolated while unwanted electrical noise is attenuated.

The peak detector converts the filtered AC waveform into a slowly varying DC voltage related to the strength of the detected magnetic field.

### 3. Embedded Control

The conditioned sensor signal is read by an Arduino Nano ESP32.

The controller determines the required motor response and modifies the PWM duty cycles applied to the motor drive circuits.

This allows the robot to correct its direction while travelling along the track.

### 4. Motor Drive

The motors are controlled using IRFZ44N power MOSFETs operating as switching devices.

Rather than controlling motor speed through inefficient linear voltage regulation, the system uses PWM.

When the PWM signal is high, the MOSFET switches on and current flows through the motor. When the PWM signal is low, the MOSFET switches off.

Changing the PWM duty cycle changes the average voltage supplied to the motor and therefore changes its speed.

This approach provides efficient and responsive motor control while reducing unnecessary power dissipation.

### 5. Differential Steering

The robot uses two independently controlled DC motors.

Steering is achieved by changing the relative speed of the left and right motors. Increasing the speed of one motor relative to the other causes the robot to turn while continuing to move forward.

## Final Single-Sensor Control Strategy

The system was originally developed using a dual-sensor architecture.

During development, both sensor channels and motor circuits had previously operated correctly. However, on race day one of the motors unexpectedly stopped operating correctly.

To ensure that we could still present a functioning autonomous robot, the control strategy was modified from the original dual-sensor system to a single-sensor approach.

This required changes to:

- Sensor configuration.
- Calibration behaviour.
- PWM parameters.
- Motor speed limits.
- Steering logic.
- Control behaviour.

The updated controller is included within this repository.

This change demonstrated the importance of adaptability during practical engineering projects, particularly when hardware does not behave as expected under final testing conditions.

## Circuit Simulation

Circuit simulation was used before and during physical implementation to investigate the behaviour of the electrical subsystems.

### Motor Drive Simulation

The MOSFET motor drive circuit was modelled to investigate PWM switching behaviour.

The simulated circuit included:

- IRFZ44N MOSFET switching devices.
- 100 Ω gate resistors.
- 10 kΩ gate pull-down resistors.
- DC motor loads.
- PWM control signals.

Simulation helped verify that varying PWM duty cycle could effectively control the average voltage supplied to the motors.

### Pick-Up Coil and Peak Detector Simulation

The sensor signal-conditioning circuit was also modelled before physical construction.

The simulation represented the small AC voltage produced by the pick-up coil and allowed the behaviour of the following stages to be investigated:

- Band-pass filtering.
- Operational amplification.
- Precision rectification.
- Peak detection.
- Signal smoothing.

LTspice was used to analyse the circuit waveforms and verify that a usable output voltage could be produced from the small sensor signal.

Simulating the system before physical construction reduced the amount of trial-and-error required during hardware development.

## Simulink and Power Electronics

## Laboratory Testing

Practical laboratory testing was a major part of the project.

### Oscilloscope

An oscilloscope was used to analyse both the sensor electronics and motor control system.

For the motor drive circuit, the oscilloscope was used to inspect the PWM control waveform and verify that the switching signal had the expected frequency, duty cycle, amplitude, and transitions.

For the peak detector circuit, multiple oscilloscope channels allowed the input and output signals to be observed simultaneously.

This helped confirm that the circuit was successfully converting the small alternating sensor signal into a usable control voltage.

### Waveform Generator

A waveform generator was used to provide repeatable electrical inputs during testing.

Square-wave signals were used when investigating switching and PWM behaviour.

Sinusoidal signals were used to simulate the signal produced by the inductive pick-up coil.

Using controlled signals allowed individual circuits to be tested before being integrated into the complete robot.

### Power Supplies

Laboratory power supplies were used during circuit development and testing to provide controlled voltage levels before operation from the robot's onboard batteries.

### Soldering

The final electronic circuits required practical soldering and assembly.

Temperature-controlled soldering equipment was used to connect components to prototyping boards while appropriate fume extraction was used during the process.

Several iterations of the electronic boards were produced before achieving a correctly functioning system.

This provided valuable experience in debugging practical circuits where faults can originate from both design errors and physical assembly issues.

## Change Management and Version Control

Git and GitHub were used to manage changes to the Arduino control software.

A particularly important change occurred when the robot transitioned from the original dual-sensor architecture to a single-sensor control strategy.

Rather than modifying the stable version immediately, a separate branch was created for the updated controller.

The workflow included:

1. Creating a dedicated development branch.
2. Modifying the sensor and control configuration.
3. Updating PWM and motor-control parameters.
4. Testing the new approach.
5. Documenting the changes using commits.
6. Creating a pull request.
7. Reviewing the modifications.
8. Merging the functioning implementation into the main branch.

This provided practical experience with professional software change-management principles and ensured that earlier functioning versions of the controller remained recoverable.

## Sustainability

Sustainability was considered across the electrical, mechanical, energy, and material aspects of the project.

### Sustainable Energy Management

PWM motor control was selected partly because switch-mode control reduces energy losses compared with linear motor-control approaches.

The MOSFETs operate mainly in their fully on or fully off states, which reduces unnecessary power dissipation.

The sensor signal-conditioning circuitry also improved energy efficiency indirectly by providing cleaner sensor information. More reliable measurements reduce unnecessary steering corrections and therefore reduce wasted motor activity.

Mechanical optimisation also contributed to energy performance by reducing friction, unnecessary mass, and the resulting motor load.

---

## Sustainable Materials Management

The battery system was identified as an important sustainability consideration.

The robot used batteries containing chemically active electrolyte materials, including alkaline electrolytes such as potassium hydroxide.

These materials are necessary for battery operation but can create environmental and safety concerns if batteries are damaged or incorrectly disposed of.

Sustainable practices considered during the project included:

- Minimising unnecessary battery replacement.
- Reducing inefficient testing.
- Operating components within appropriate electrical limits.
- Protecting the robot from physical damage.
- Correct disposal and recycling of batteries.
- Reusing existing components where possible.

---

## Engineering Design for Circular Economy

Circular economy principles were considered using the 4R framework:

### Reduce

Material and energy consumption were reduced through:

- Efficient PWM motor control.
- Reduced unnecessary casing.
- Lightweight mechanical construction.
- Efficient sensor processing.
- Reduced unnecessary motor activity.

### Reuse

Several major components were designed to remain reusable, including:

- Arduino Nano ESP32.
- DC motors.
- Electrical connectors.
- Sensor circuitry.
- Mechanical chassis components.

Pin headers and removable connections were preferred where appropriate so components could be disconnected without damaging them.

Existing mechanical components from previous project cohorts were also reused.

### Recycle

Where components could no longer be reused, consideration was given to appropriate recycling routes for electronic components, batteries, metals, and plastics.

### Repair

The modular architecture of the robot made individual subsystems easier to repair or replace without discarding the entire system.

## Life Cycle Assessment

The project considered the environmental impact of the robot across its complete lifecycle.

The main stages considered were:

1. Raw material extraction and processing.
2. Component manufacturing.
3. Assembly.
4. Distribution.
5. Use.
6. Maintenance and end-of-life.

Materials included copper, silicon, electronic components, plastics, motors, batteries, and circuit-board materials.

The assessment indicated that manufacturing and raw material processing represented a much greater environmental impact than the short operational period of the robot.

For the recorded testing period, the operational electrical energy consumption was extremely small compared with the embodied carbon associated with producing the electronic hardware.

This strengthened the case for reducing material use and reusing components wherever possible.

## Key Engineering Lessons

One of the most important lessons from this project was that successful engineering requires more than producing a design that works once.

The robot required continuous integration between sensing, electronics, software, mechanics, and power systems. A problem in any one subsystem could affect the complete system.

The unexpected motor issue on race day demonstrated the importance of having adaptable control software and being able to rapidly modify a system while preserving earlier versions of the design.

The project also reinforced the value of simulation, structured decision making, practical testing, documentation, version control, and teamwork when developing multidisciplinary engineering systems.
